# How Lighthouse Keepers Spent the Night

Tonight, we'll spend a long, quiet night with the people who kept the lights burning along the coast. There's nothing you need to remember, and nowhere you need to be. Just the sea, the lamp, and the slow turning of the hours.

In 1888, a lighthouse on the coast of Maine was a small world of its own. A tower of white stone, a keeper's cottage, a shed for oil and wicks, and a path down to the rocks. The nearest town was a few miles away, across water that could turn gray and restless in an afternoon.

The keeper's day began when most people's ended. At dusk, he climbed the spiral stairs, one hundred and twelve of them, carrying a can of oil and a small brass lantern. At the top, he trimmed the wick, polished the great glass lens, and lit the lamp.

# The First Watch

Fact number one. The lamp had to be tended every few hours through the night. A wick burned unevenly, and an uneven flame meant a dimmer light for the ships far out at sea.

Fact number two. Keepers kept a logbook, and most entries were wonderfully dull. Wind from the west. Light rain after midnight. Lamp trimmed at 2 AM. Sea calm. [pause 2s]

Fact number three. Many keepers brought their families. Children did their lessons at the kitchen table, and learned to fall asleep to the sound of waves against the rocks, and the soft tick of the clockwork that turned the lens.

In the small hours, the keeper might sit by the stove with a cup of tea, listening. The wind moved around the tower. The kettle settled. Somewhere below, the sea breathed in, and out, and in again.
